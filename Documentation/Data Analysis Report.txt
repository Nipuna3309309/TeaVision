1. Introduction 
1.1. Background: 
Sri Lanka’s plantation economy remains strongly supported by the tea sector, which 
contributes substantially to export revenue and rural livelihoods, particularly through 
smallholder producers. However, decision-making across the tea value chain is 
frequently constrained by manual inspection practices, delayed reporting, and limited 
access to field-level analytics. These limitations reduce consistency in quality 
assessment, weaken early intervention in crop risks, and restrict market 
responsiveness. 
Four operational gaps are commonly observed. First, freshness grading and tea-type 
identification are often performed manually, resulting in variability across inspectors 
and locations. Second, yield planning is affected by uncertainty in weather, field 
conditions, and management practices, limiting the ability to anticipate crop output 
and optimize resource allocation. Third, demand estimation and auction planning 
typically rely on historical trends and expert judgment, which can be insufficient under 
market volatility. Fourth, disease identification and treatment guidance are often 
delayed due to limited expert availability, leading to avoidable yield losses and quality 
degradation. 
To address these issues, a mobile-based, data-driven decision support application is 
being developed as a student-led data science project, with a focus on accessibility for 
smallholder communities. The system is designed to integrate (i) automated freshness 
grading and tea-type classification, (ii) predictive yield analytics, (iii) market demand 
forecasting for smarter auction planning, and (iv) AI-based tea leaf disease and pest 
detection with treatment guidance. By enabling rapid field-level assessments and 
actionable recommendations, the proposed solution aims to improve consistency, 
efficiency, and scalability of tea-sector decision-making in Sri Lanka. 
 
 
1.2. Research Problem: 
The Sri Lankan tea industry, though vital to the economy, struggles with productivity, 
quality, and sustainability. Farmers face inconsistent plucking, quality loss, and 
undetected disease and pest outbreaks, while factories and auction stakeholders

1.3. Objectives: 
1) To develop an automated tea leaf freshness grading and tea type classification 
module using computer vision techniques, reducing subjectivity and variability 
associated with manual inspection. 
2) To implement tea yield analytics and digitization module that converts

2.1. Data Collection 
 
To build a real-world dataset, we visited smallholder tea estates and collected freshly 
plucked leaves directly from the field. For each sample, the leaves were spread in a 
single layer on a clean, light-coloured (white) cloth to create a consistent, low-noise 
background. Images were captured outdoors under daylight using our dedicated data-

real plantation images of major locally relevant tea leaf diseases, pests, and invasive

Data source 1: 
 
The self-collected Tea Leaf Image Dataset currently contains 275 original images augmented 
to 1,000 samples. The data collection infrastructure is designed for longitudinal scalability

through a batch management system with unique batch identifiers 
(BATCH_YYYYMMDD_HHMMSS), sequential image indexing for traceability, and JSON 
sidecar metadata files that enable automated ingestion into database systems. Automated 
quality gates maintain dataset consistency as collection scales across multiple operators, 
with rejection logging identifying systematic capture issues for operator training. The 
TeaVision mobile application supports configurable capture resolutions (2 MP to 12 MP+)

splits for incremental learning as the dataset grows. 
 
Data source 2: 
 
The Pruning Cycle Yield Logbook dataset currently includes 175 images across two 
mid‑country estates. In this dataset there are two distinct pruning‑cycle templates, each 
corresponding to a different plantation, and within each plantation the same template is 
reused for all estates. Together these two templates cover approximately 35 estates, so 
once the digitization pipeline is configured for each template, it can scale by simply ingesting 
additional estate yield books without changing the extraction logic. 
 
Data source 3: 
 
The monthly yield forecasting dataset consists of 5,821 field‑wise records with 22 variables 
from a single mid‑country estate over 2012–2025, providing enough historical coverage and 
management variation to train and validate estate‑level monthly yield prediction models. 
Each new year’s data can be appended as additional rows to this dataset as it becomes 
available. 
 
Data source 4:

compresses the dataset into manageable time-series sequences per grade/elevation. The 
system is scalable by adding new yearly auction files and retraining models periodically.

3.3. Feature extraction 
 
 
Objective 1: Tea Leaf Freshness Grading 
A total of 25 hand-crafted features were extracted from each leaf image across four 
categories: 
Colour Features (6): Mean Hue, Mean Saturation, Mean Value (HSV colour space over leaf-
masked pixels), Greenness Index (composite metric: (1 − |meanHue − 120| / 60) × 
meanSaturation), Brownness Index (oxidation indicator for hue ∈ [20°, 60°]), and Colour 
Uniformity (1 / (1 + hueVariance / 1000)). 
Texture Features (2): Blur Score (Laplacian variance × 10 on 400px-width scaled image) and 
Background Variance (standard deviation of luminance in peripheral 15% edge regions). 
Morphological Features (6): Leaf Area (pixels and cm²), Leaf Width (cm), Leaf Height (cm), 
Leaf Coverage (%), and Aspect Ratio — derived from binary segmentation masks with 
optional QR-code calibration for real-world measurements. 
Quality Metrics (4): Resolution (MP), Brightness (mean luminance), Glare Percentage, and 
Segmentation Confidence (0.9 for ML-based, 0.7 for colour-based fallback). 
Segmentation uses a dual approach: a TensorFlow Lite model (256×256 input, softmax 
threshold 0.5) as the primary method, with a colour-based fallback using adaptive brightness 
thresholds and greenish-tint validation for devices without ML acceleration.

Objective 3: Tea Market Demand Forecasting and Smart Auction Planning 
 
A total of 30 engineered time-series and market-intelligence features were extracted for each 
Grade × Elevation × Week record to forecast demand signals (price pressure + buyer activity) 
and support smart auction planning. Features were derived across five categories: 
Market Price Dynamics :Lagged and trend-based price indicators were computed to capture 
short-term and seasonal market movement: Price(t−1), Price(t−2), Price(t−4), 4 -week Rolling 
Mean, 8-week Rolling Mean, 4 -week Rolling Std (volatility), Week -over-Week Price Change 
(%), and Momentum Index (rolling mean of WoW change over last 3 weeks). 
Supply & Volume Pressure Features:  Auction supply signals were modelled using quantity -
based indicators: Offered Quantity(t−1), (t−2), (t−4), 4-week Rolling Mean Quantity, Quantity 
Shock Flag (1 if quantity > mean + 1.5×std), Scarcity Index (1 / (1 + normalized quantity)) and 
Grade-Mix Share (grade quantity / total weekly quantity within the elevation category). 
Buyer Demand & Competition Features:  Demand strength was captured using buyer -side 
proxies: Active Buyer Count, Top-3 Buyer Share, Buyer Concentration (HHI), Competition Ratio 
(buyers / lots), Repeat-Buyer Rate (buyers appearing in both current and previous week), and 
Bid Intensity Proxy (normalized price increase under limited supply conditions). 
Demand Target Construction (Forecast Label)  : For modelling demand, the target variable 
was defined as a  Demand Pressure Score, computed using a weighted combination of price 
uplift, buyer participation, and supply scarcity (higher prices with lower offered quantities and 
higher buyer competition imply stronger demand). This demand signal is then used to 
recommend optimal offering quantities, grade prioritization, and reserve price guidance for 
smart auction planning.

4.2. Challenges Faced During Data Analysis: 
 
The analysis of our datasets presented several challenges, primarily during the data 
gathering and preprocessing phases. The key challenges encountered include: 
 
1. Data Collection Challenges 
 
• Freshness grade labelling needed domain expert judgment, which introduces 
subjectivity. Different assessors may give different grades to borderline samples. To 
reduce this, all 275 original labels were manually audited for consistency. 
 
• Early tea leaf collection was biased toward fresh samples because field visits coincided 
with recent plucking. Fresh leaves were easy to find, while aged or damaged samples 
had to be collected deliberately. 
 
• Although public datasets were available, collecting data that matches Sri Lankan tea 
estate conditions was difficult. Field data collection required consistent lighting, camera 
angles, and backgrounds.  
• In some cases, certain disease, pest, or invasive species categories had fewer samples, 
creating class imbalance. 
• Invasive species images also required careful collection because plants were often mixed 
with surrounding weeds. 
• Auction data came from several Excel files (2023–2025). Even though the columns were 
mostly the same, small formatting differences (extra spaces, numbers stored as text, 

   
 
BSc (Hons) in Information Technology Specializing Data Science 
 
 
Research Project - IT4010                                                         Data Analysis Report  
 
  16 
naming differences) had to be cleaned before merging, and that can hide small 
inconsistencies. 
• Real demand data (actual bids, buyer competition, order books, broker negotiations) 
were not available. So demand had to be estimated using indirect signals like price 
changes and supply shortages, which is less accurate than having true buyer-side data. 
• Some tea grades do not appear every week in each elevation (Low/Medium/High). This 
creates short or broken time series for those grades, making forecasting harder and 
forcing us to remove grades with too few data points. 
• The dataset uses SALE NO instead of normal calendar weeks. Converting SALE NO into 
dates requires assumptions and careful handling across year changes, and small timing 
mismatches can affect alignment with external data. 
 
2. Data Quality and Variability 
 
• Different smartphone cameras reproduce colors differently and use different white balance 
and exposure settings. This affects how well the model works across devices, especially for 
colour-based features like greenness and brownness indices. 
• Outdoor lighting changes (shadows from trees, cloud cover, time-of-day shifts) cause 
brightness to vary by about ±30 units, so preprocessing normalization is needed before 
extracting colour features. 
• The datasets contained variations in image quality, background noise, and symptom 
appearance. Tea leaf diseases and pest damage often look similar, such as Blister Blight and 
Brown Blight share similar visual symptoms, which made classification more challenging. 
• Auction and yield datasets also required careful checking due to inconsistent formatting 
across sources. 
 
3. Preprocessing and Data Cleaning Issues 
 
Overlapping leaves, curled edges, and shadows create unclear boundaries during segmentation, 
which affects the accuracy of leaf measurements like area, width, and height. 
Session-based data splitting is needed to prevent data leakage. Images from the same capture 
session share lighting, angles, and leaf arrangement, so randomly splitting across sessions would give 
misleadingly high performance numbers. 
• Several preprocessing steps were needed, such as image resizing, normalization, 
augmentation, and label verification.  
• Background complexity and early-stage infections sometimes reduced symptom 
visibility, affecting feature learning.  
• Seasonal prediction data also required consistency checks across climatic variables and 
risk-level distributions.

5. Computational and Training Limitations 
The current dataset of only 275 original images (augmented to 1,000) is relatively small for deep 
learning, which limits how well the model can generalize to new, unseen data. Continued data

• Limited hardware availability increased training time and restricted the number of
